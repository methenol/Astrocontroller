# 1.Some Basics to Learn

Before observing or photographing the lunar eclipse, it is helpful to understand how a lunar eclipse happens and what you may see during the event.

## 1.1 What Is a Lunar Eclipse?

A lunar eclipse occurs when the Earth moves between the Sun and the Moon, causing the Earth’s shadow to fall on the Moon.

Unlike a solar eclipse, a lunar eclipse can be safely viewed with the naked eye. It may also be visible across a large part of the Earth wherever the Moon is above the horizon.

The August 2026 event is a **partial lunar eclipse**. At maximum eclipse, most of the Moon will be covered by the Earth’s dark shadow, making it visually similar to a near-total lunar eclipse.

## 1.2 Key Stages of a Lunar Eclipse

A partial lunar eclipse usually includes the following stages:

  * **Penumbral Eclipse Begins:** The Moon enters the Earth’s outer shadow. The brightness change may be difficult to notice.

  * **Partial Eclipse Begins:** Part of the Moon enters the Earth’s inner shadow, and a dark “bite” begins to appear.

  * **Maximum Eclipse:** The Moon reaches the deepest stage of the eclipse. At this time, the eclipsed part of the Moon may appear **dark red, orange, or copper-colored**.

Tips: Why does the Moon turn red?

During a lunar eclipse, the Earth blocks direct sunlight from reaching the Moon. However, some sunlight passes through the Earth’s atmosphere and bends into the Earth’s shadow. Most blue light is scattered away, while more red and orange light reaches the Moon, giving the eclipsed part its reddish color.

  * Partial Eclipse Ends: The Moon leaves the Earth’s inner shadow.

  * Penumbral Eclipse Ends: The Moon completely leaves the Earth’s outer shadow, marking the end of the eclipse.

For observation and photography, the most noticeable period is from **Partial Eclipse Begins** to **Partial Eclipse Ends** , with **Maximum Eclipse** being the key moment to capture.

# 2\. Date, Time and Visibility

## 2.1 Event Overview

August 2026 features two major eclipses in the same eclipse season: a **total solar eclipse on August 12** and a **partial lunar eclipse on August 27**.

The eclipse will be visible from the **eastern Pacific, North America, South America, Europe, and Africa**. The Americas will have some of the best views of the event, while observers in parts of Europe and Africa may see only part of the eclipse near the moonset.

# 5\. How to Capture the Partial Lunar Eclipse

For the 2026 partial lunar eclipse, we recommend using **Time Lapse + AE** as the main shooting method to record the entire eclipse. You can also capture stacked photos at key moments to preserve more lunar detail.

Before starting the eclipse capture, complete the basic Moon shooting setup: set up the telescope, connect the device, locate and focus on the Moon, and enable **Moon Track**.

Once Moon Track is active, follow the steps below to record the eclipse.

## 5.1 Record the Eclipse with Time Lapse

Recommended shooting method: Time Lapse + AE

### Step 1. Switch to Time Lapse

After Moon Track is enabled, switch the shooting mode to **Time Lapse**.

### Step 2. Set the Interval

Set the **interval between 10 and 30 seconds**.

  * **30-second interval:** fewer images and easier file management.

  * **10-second interval:** records more frames and produces smoother motion in the final time lapse.

For the full eclipse duration of about 5 hours and 38 minutes, a 30-second interval produces approximately a 22-second time lapse at 30 fps, while a 10-second interval produces approximately 68 seconds.

### Step 3. Turn on AE

We recommend keeping **AE** on during the eclipse. As the brightness of the Moon changes, AE automatically adjusts the exposure to help maintain a properly exposed lunar image.

As long as the lunar detail looks good in the preview, no additional adjustment is needed.

### Step 4. Keep Filter and White Balance Consistent

Keep the **Filter** and **White Balance** consistent throughout the eclipse to maintain a consistent look across the entire sequence.

### Step 5. Start the Time Lapse

Check the preview one more time, then start the Time Lapse.

During the eclipse, you only need to occasionally check the preview to make sure the Moon remains properly exposed and tracked.

> ### Tips: Manual Exposure
> 
> We recommend using **AE** throughout the eclipse. If the eclipsed part of the Moon still appears too dark near maximum eclipse, you can temporarily increase **Exposure** or **Gain** based on the preview.
> 
> You can gradually increase Exposure based on the preview, up to around **2 seconds** if necessary. If the image remains too dark, then increase Gain slightly.
> 
> As the Moon becomes brighter again after maximum eclipse, **switch back to AE** so the exposure can automatically adapt to the changing brightness.

> **Reference:** Start from Gain 0 on DWARF 3 and increase gradually if needed; start from Gain 40 on DWARF Mini and increase gradually if needed.

## 5.2 Capture a Stacked Photo at Key Moments

Time Lapse is recommended for recording the entire eclipse. If you also want to preserve a clear still image of a key stage, you can temporarily switch to **Photo** during the Time Lapse.

Recommended moments include:

  * Partial Eclipse Begins

  * Near Maximum Eclipse

  * Maximum Eclipse

  * When the Moon begins to brighten again

### Step 1. Stop the Time Lapse

When the Moon reaches the stage you want to capture, stop the current Time Lapse.

### Step 2. Keep Moon Track Active

Keep **Moon Track** active. There is no need to locate the Moon again.

### Step 3. Switch to Photo

Switch the shooting method from Time Lapse to **Photo**.

Check the preview and make sure the Moon is properly exposed before shooting.

### Step 4. Tap Shoot

Tap **Shoot** and wait for the telescope to complete the capture and stacking process.

When processing is complete, you will get a finished stacked lunar photo.

### Step 5. Return to Time Lapse

After capturing the photo, switch back to **Time Lapse** and continue recording the eclipse.

Before resuming, check that Moon Track is still active and confirm that the preview looks normal.

> **Important:** Do not reposition the Moon when switching between Time Lapse and Photo. This helps keep the framing consistent throughout the eclipse.

You can repeat this process whenever you want to capture another important stage.

## 5.3 Finish the Eclipse Capture

Continue the Time Lapse until the main partial eclipse phase ends.

If you captured several Time Lapse clips while taking stacked photos in between, simply arrange the clips in chronological order during editing.

This workflow gives you both:

  * **A complete record of the eclipse with Time Lapse**

  * **Clear stacked photos from key stages**
